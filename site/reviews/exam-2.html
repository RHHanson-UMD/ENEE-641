CH 23Minimum spanning trees and cuts

Source inspected: Chapter23Oct8b-2026, slides 1–14; syllabus Oct. 27

  • A spanning tree is connected, acyclic, and has |V|−1 edges. Prove the edge count by removing a leaf and applying induction.
  • For a cut respecting a known-safe edge set, a minimum-weight crossing edge is safe (Theorem 23.1 exchange argument).
  • Kruskal sorts candidate edges and avoids cycles using disjoint sets; Prim grows one tree across light cut edges. Compare their priority and union-find costs.
Practice: For a 4-cycle of unit edges plus one diagonal of weight 5, find an MST of cost 3 and explain why the heavy diagonal is unnecessary.
Proof prompt: If an MST omits a light cut edge, add it and identify a crossing edge to remove without increasing weight.

CH 22Graph representations, BFS, DFS, and topology

Source inspected: Chapter22Oct15-2026, slides 1–21; syllabus Oct. 29 and Nov. 3

  • Adjacency lists use O(|V|+|E|) space; matrices use O(|V|²) and answer adjacency membership queries in O(1).
  • BFS computes edge-count distances δ(s,v) with a queue; prove distances and O(V+E) traversal by charging work to vertices/edges.
  • DFS tracks discovery d(v) and finish f(v), with directed tree/back/forward/cross edges. Topological ordering is possible for DAGs; the instructor prefers zero-indegree peeling as an intuitive construction.
  • For undirected connected graphs, understand DFS articulation points, bridges, low-link intuition, edge-based biconnected components, and the even-degree characterization of Euler tours.
Practice: In a path a–b–c, b is an articulation point and both edges are bridges; explain why removing them changes connectivity.
Proof prompt: Why does repeatedly taking an indegree-zero vertex fail to produce a full order on a directed cycle?
CH 24Single-source shortest paths

Source inspected: Chapter24Oct29-2026, slides 1–9; syllabus Nov. 5 excludes §24.4

  • Directed weighted graph and δ(u,v) notation. A shortest path has shortest subpaths; understand negative-cycle caveats.
  • RELAX(u,v) can decrease d(v) when d(u)+w(u,v)<d(v). Bellman–Ford repeats edge relaxation |V|−1 rounds and uses another round to detect a reachable negative cycle.
  • For DAG shortest/longest paths, process zero-indegree vertices in topological order and relax outgoing edges; the lecture treats separate sorting as potentially redundant.
  • Dijkstra requires nonnegative edge weights. Prove finalized distances correct via the first vertex outside the finalized set on a hypothetical shorter path.
Counterexample: A graph with s→a weight 2, s→b weight 5, and b→a weight −10 shows why nonnegative weights are a required precondition for Dijkstra's standard finalization proof.
Practice: Show how |V|−1 Bellman–Ford passes suffice for a shortest simple path with at most |V|−1 edges.
CH 25Floyd–Warshall all-pairs shortest paths

Source inspected: Chapter25Nov3-2026, slides 1–7; syllabus Nov. 10

  • The lecture's stage invariant: after iteration k, dᵢⱼ^(k) is the shortest i→j path whose intermediate vertices belong to {1,…,k}, assuming no negative cycle.
  • Update dᵢⱼ^(k)=min(dᵢⱼ^(k−1),dᵢₖ^(k−1)+dₖⱼ^(k−1)); explain why only those two cases cover a shortest path.
  • Time O(n³); distinguish distance matrices from predecessor/path reconstruction. Source Figure 25.1 is in image-based artwork, not verified from extracted text.
Practice: With edges a→b 3, b→c 2 and a→c 9, the b stage improves a→c from 9 to 5.
Boundary: A negative cycle invalidates the conventional finite shortest-path interpretation.
CH 34NP, verification, and polynomial-time reductions

Source inspected: Chapter34Nov5-2026, slides 1–40; syllabus Nov. 12–19

  • Separate optimization from decision, integer magnitude n from its binary encoding length, and membership in P from polynomial-time verification in NP.
  • NP-complete means in NP and NP-hard; prove a new language L NP-hard by reducing a known NP-complete L′ to L—not the reverse.
  • Know the five-part lecture proof recipe: membership in NP; known hard source; polynomial mapping; yes⇔yes; mapping time bound.
  • The lecture follows CIRCUIT-SAT→SAT→3-CNF-SAT, then CLIQUE→VERTEX-COVER→HAM-CYCLE→TSP and a branch to SUBSET-SUM. Retain source graph/clauses and correct direction when tracing.
  • Hamiltonian cycle differs from Euler tour; TSP decision reduction assigns zero cost to edges of the input graph and one to absent edges, with threshold 0.
Practice: Prove a polynomial transformation A≤ₚB allows a B solver to decide A; explain why the reverse reduction does not prove B is hard.
Counterexample: An algorithm polynomial in a numerical value may be exponential in its bit-length.
CH 35Approximation algorithms and subset-sum FPTAS

Source inspected: Chapter35Nov17-2026, slides 1–15; actually introduced by Nov. 19; syllabus plans review and §§35.1–35.2 Nov. 24

  • Ratio convention from slides: max(C/C*,C*/C)≤ρ(n). Distinguish minimization versus maximization and worst-case guarantees.
  • APPROX-VERTEX-COVER chooses an uncovered edge and includes both endpoints; selected edges form a maximal matching, yielding a factor-2 cover.
  • Metric TSP: MST cost≤optimal Hamiltonian tour cost; double MST then shortcut, relying on triangle inequality, to obtain a 2-approximation.
  • Set cover greedy repeatedly picks an effective set; the lecture example has 12 elements and six sets.
  • For subset-sum optimization, choose the largest sum ≤t; TRIM prunes nearby sums with δ and the FPTAS uses ε>0. Source slide example S={104,102,201,101}, t=308, ε=0.4.
Practice: For S={104,102,201,101}, t=308, identify a feasible near-target subset and compare it with exact optimum using the interactive tool.
Proof prompt: Explain exactly where the metric TSP argument breaks if the triangle inequality is absent.

Independent exam-style design practice

These are new questions, not reproduced exams or homework. For each, give an efficient algorithm, preconditions, complexity and proof.

DESIGN 1Connectivity under vertex removal

Given an undirected road network and a vertex v, determine whether removing v separates any other two vertices. Explain how DFS timestamps and low-link values avoid rerunning a full search for each candidate.

Hint: distinguish a DFS root from a nonroot, and justify each separating child subtree.

DESIGN 2Longest paths in a DAG

For a directed acyclic network with real edge weights (including negative values), compute the maximum total weight of a path from a designated source to every reachable vertex. Explain why processing vertices in topological order works, and why Dijkstra's nonnegative-weight argument does not apply.

Hint: topological order ensures predecessor states are processed first; represent unreachable vertices explicitly.

DESIGN 3Two incompatible TSP arguments

Compare the Chapter 34 zero/one cost reduction for the NP-complete decision problem with Chapter 35's MST preorder metric approximation. Explain which requires triangle inequality and why they prove different claims.

Exam 1 — comprehensive review

Foundations, recursion, sorting and order statistics through balanced trees, dynamic programming and greedy strategies. Includes the source lecture's charging arguments and exact examples.

Lecture topics and proof checklist

Open each chapter to review assumptions, algorithms, proof obligations, and practice. Mark yourself ready only when you can reproduce the argument without notes.

CH 01Asymptotic notation, induction, and recurrences

Source inspected: Class1Sept1-2026, slides 11–14, 20–26; syllabus Sept. 1–10

  • Prove Big-O, Ω, Θ statements using constants and a threshold, rather than comparing only a few values.
  • Expand T(n)=2T(n/2)+n by levels; show log₂n levels each contribute n (for n a power of two).
  • Use recursion trees, substitution, and the Master Theorem with its hypotheses; identify what the input size actually measures.
Practice: Show T(n)=3T(n/4)+n² has Θ(n²) growth. The nonrecursive n² term dominates n^(log₄3); a geometric recursion-tree sum is bounded by a constant multiple of n².
Counterexample: An O(n²) upper bound alone does not establish Θ(n²). Supply a corresponding Ω(n²) argument before claiming tightness.
CH 02Sorting and randomized quicksort

Source inspected: Class1Sept1-2026, slides 15–18, 27–30, 34–43; syllabus Chapters 2 and 7

  • Explain and prove the PARTITION loop invariant; identify pivot placement and the work charged to each processed element.
  • MERGE runs in linear time on two sorted subarrays; merge sort satisfies 2T(n/2)+Θ(n).
  • Quicksort has quadratic worst-case behavior; the randomized analysis uses indicators for pairwise element comparisons and linearity of expectation.
Practice: Trace PARTITION on [7,2,5,1,6] with last-item pivot 6, keeping the invariant that the processed left partition is at most the pivot.
Proof prompt: Why can any two ranks in randomized quicksort be compared at most once? Explain which pivot is the first selected from their rank interval.
CH 09Minimum, selection, and median-of-medians

Source inspected: Class1Sept1-2026, slides 31–33, 44–53; syllabus Sept. 15

  • Finding a minimum uses n−1 comparisons; min/max in pairs uses roughly 3n/2 comparisons.
  • Randomized-SELECT has linear expected time but quadratic worst-case time.
  • Deterministic SELECT groups elements into fives, chooses a median of medians, and discards a constant fraction to achieve worst-case O(n).
Practice: For 17 elements, partition into groups of five plus a final smaller group; explain why a good pivot discards enough elements even though the small final group complicates exact counts.
Proof prompt: Explain why T(n) ≤ T(⌈n/5⌉)+T(7n/10+6)+O(n) supports a linear-time upper bound for sufficiently large n.
CH 06Heaps, heapsort, and priority queues

Source inspected: Chapter6Sept15-2026, slides 1–15

  • The slides use 1-based indexing: parent(i)=⌊i/2⌋, left(i)=2i, right(i)=2i+1.
  • MAX-HEAPIFY runs in Θ(h) at a node of height h; BUILD-MAX-HEAP runs in O(n), justified by summing height-weighted node counts.
  • HEAPSORT uses Θ(n log n) time. Distinguish MAXIMUM, EXTRACT-MAX, INCREASE-KEY, and INSERT.
Slide-seeded exercise: Trace BUILD-MAX-HEAP and sorting on [9,1,3,14,10,2,8,16,7,4]. The initial array is explicitly in the lecture; record every swap before comparing with a tool.
Proof prompt: Explain why n/2 leaves cost zero heapify work, while progressively fewer nodes have large heights.
CH 08Noncomparison sorting, stability, and indicator expectations

Source inspected: Chapter8Sept17-2026, slides 1–10

  • Comparison sorts require Ω(n log n) worst-case comparisons through a binary decision-tree argument with n! leaves.
  • COUNTING-SORT assumes integer keys 0..k and takes Θ(n+k); stability is essential to least-significant-digit RADIX-SORT.
  • RADIX-SORT takes Θ(d(n+k)); BUCKET-SORT's expected Θ(n) analysis depends on the distribution assumptions and E[nᵢ²]=2−1/n for the lecture's n-bucket uniform model.
Slide-inspired practice: For n=40,000 and keys in 0..999,999,999, explain why radix base n needs two digits: n² exceeds 10⁹.
Counterexample: If all elements land in one bucket, insertion sort within that bucket can be quadratic.
CH 11Hash tables: chaining, universal hashing, and probing

Source inspected: Chapter11Sept22-2026, slides 1–22 (read from original PowerPoint slide XML after text-index extraction returned empty).

  • Direct addressing uses one slot per possible key; hashing maps a larger universe to m slots and introduces collisions.
  • In chaining, α=n/m is the mean chain length. Under simple uniform hashing, expected unsuccessful search takes Θ(1+α), and successful search has the same asymptotic expected bound under the lecture's random-element assumption.
  • Division method: h(k)=k mod m. Multiplication method: floor(m·fractional(kA)). Understand why choice of m and key distribution matter.
  • Universal hashing chooses a random function from a family such that collision probability for two distinct keys is at most 1/m. The lecture family is hₐ,ᵦ(k)=((ak+b) mod p) mod m for prime p>m.
  • Open addressing: linear probing, quadratic probing, double hashing, primary/secondary clustering, probe cycle coverage, and deletion tombstones.
  • Perfect hashing for static keys uses a two-level table and secondary bucket space nⱼ² to achieve worst-case constant search with appropriate chosen functions.
Exact slide 12 calculation: h₃,₄(8) for p=17, m=6 equals ((3·8+4) mod 17) mod 6=5.
Slide 17–18 trace: Insert 89, 18, 49, 58, 9 into m=10 under linear and quadratic probing. Use the hashing explorer and explain which probe sequences revisit slots.
CH 12Binary search trees: traversal, search, and delete cases

Source inspected: Chapter12Sept24-2026, slides 1–7

  • Slide BST invariant allows left subtree keys ≤ node key and right subtree keys ≥ node key; duplicates are possible.
  • INORDER-TREE-WALK visits keys in sorted order and takes O(n). Search, min/max, successor, insertion, and deletion depend on tree height.
  • Deletion cases: leaf removal; one child splice; two children with successor as immediate right child; or successor deeper in right subtree.
Slide-seeded facts: Both source trees produce inorder output 2,3,5,5,6,7,8; one has height 2 and the other 4. Explain how structure changes search cost without changing sorted output.
Practice: For a BST containing 10,5,15,12,18, remove 10. Explain the successor transplant and verify inorder ordering.
CH 13Red-black trees: invariants and fixup cases

Source inspected: Chapter13Sept29-2026, slides 1–18

  • Know root-black, black NIL leaves, no red-red parent/child, and equal black height along paths from each node.
  • Explain the O(log n) height argument using black height and the red-node restriction.
  • Trace insertion: red uncle recolor; black uncle with inside child rotation; black uncle with outside child rotation. Deletion diagrams are in the source and require careful case-specific verification.
Practice: Insert 10,20,30 into an initially empty red-black tree. Explain the rotation and recoloring restoring the invariants.
Proof prompt: Why can a red-black tree never have a root-to-leaf path more than twice the length of a shortest root-to-leaf path?
CH 14Augmentation: order statistics and interval trees

Source inspected: Chapter14Oct1b-2026, slides 1–7

  • Store size(x) as subtree node count to support OS-SELECT and rank queries along red-black-tree paths.
  • For closed intervals, overlap means neither interval lies completely to one side; maintain max high-endpoint in each subtree.
  • In INTERVAL-SEARCH, if current interval fails to overlap, test max[left[x]] ≥ low(query) to choose the left subtree; justify why skipping a subtree is safe.
Practice: Given subtree sizes 3 and 5 around a root, find the root rank and the side containing rank 6.
Boundary case: Closed intervals [1,3] and [3,5] overlap at 3; changing to open intervals would alter the condition.
CH 15Dynamic programming: matrix chains and LCS

Source inspected: Chapter15Oct6-2026, slides 1–7

  • Matrix chains: m[i,i]=0; m[i,j]=min over i≤k<j of m[i,k]+m[k+1,j]+p[i−1]p[k]p[j].
  • The lecture source dimensions are A₁ 30×35, A₂ 35×15, A₃ 15×5, A₄ 5×10, A₅ 10×20, A₆ 20×25. Its example m[2,5]=7125.
  • LCS recurrence matches last symbols diagonally or takes max of two prefix states. Demonstrate optimal substructure and induction over i+j.
Practice: For matrices of dimensions 10×30,30×5,5×60, compare ((A₁A₂)A₃) cost 4500 with (A₁(A₂A₃)) cost 27000.
Practice: Find an LCS of ABCBDAB and BDCABA; verify the returned subsequence occurs in both in order.
CH 16Greedy choice: interval scheduling and Huffman codes

Source inspected: Chapter16Oct8-2026, slides 1–8

  • Activity-selection chooses earliest finish time among compatible intervals; activities starting at the preceding finish time are compatible.
  • Prove the exchange step: replacing the first chosen activity in some optimal schedule with the earliest-finishing compatible activity never reduces available time for later choices.
  • Huffman joins the two least frequent characters at each step; the objective is weighted external path length ∑f(c)d(c). Explain the sibling lemma and optimal substructure.
Practice: From (start,finish)=(0,4),(1,2),(2,5),(4,6), select a maximum compatible subset with earliest-finish greedy.
Counterexample: Choosing the shortest-duration interval rather than the earliest-finishing compatible interval need not maximize the count.
